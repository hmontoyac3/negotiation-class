Theory · Harvard Negotiation Project · 1981
PRINCIPLED NEGOTIATION
Fisher, R. & Ury, W. — Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In
1
Separate people from the problem
The relationship and the deal are two different things. Don't let one poison the other.
From the orangesMost pairs turned competitive early — treating the other company as an obstacle rather than a partner. But the conflict wasn't personal. You wanted different parts of the same fruit. The person wasn't the problem. The assumption was.
In real lifeHR is not your enemy. They have a budget constraint. Attack the gap between the offer and the market — not the person holding the offer. "Based on market rates I was expecting €2,900" lands differently than "your offer is too low."
↓ click to expand
2
Focus on interests, not positions
A position is what you say you want. An interest is why you want it. Positions conflict. Interests often don't.
From the orangesBoth cards said "we want the harvest." Same position. PharmaOne needed the rind. FreshPress needed the juice. Perfectly compatible — hidden behind an identical-sounding demand. The moment someone asked "what exactly do you need it for?" the deal was done.
In real lifeWhen someone says no to your budget request, don't argue the number. Ask: "What would make a yes possible?" The answer is almost never "less money." It's usually timing, approval chain, optics, or a different line item entirely.
↓ click to expand
3
Generate options for mutual gain
Don't negotiate over one solution. Invent options first. Expand the pie before you divide it.
From the orangesThe win-win was there from minute one — split the harvest, rinds to one, juice to the other, both get 100%. Instead, most pairs fought over who owned the whole. They were dividing a pie they could have doubled by asking one question first.
In real lifeSalary has more variables than salary: start date, remote days, training budget, title, review timing. Trade across issues. The candidate who proposes a flexible start date gets the extra €200 per month. The one who only argues salary stays stuck at the same number.
↓ click to expand
4
Insist on objective criteria
When interests genuinely clash on one issue, use external standards — not willpower — to resolve it.
From the orangesIf both companies truly needed the same part of the orange, the question becomes: what's fair? Market price for the harvest. Volume precedent from last year. An independent valuation. Something neither side invented — so neither side loses face by accepting it.
In real life"Market rates for this role in Milan are €2,800–€3,200 — that's my reference point, not a personal demand." Objective criteria depersonalise the ask. You're not arguing with HR. You're both looking at the same data and asking: what does the evidence say is fair?

Why this framework still dominates 40 years later

Fisher & Ury published this in 1981. It became the foundation of modern negotiation — used in international diplomacy, M&A, labour disputes, and every salary conversation happening right now.

You just ran the orange exercise without knowing it existed. The pairs who found the win-win used principles 2 and 3 instinctively. The ones who didn't got locked in position battles. Same information. Same scenario. The only difference was which question they asked first.

The End Game
CLOSING THE DEAL
1Summarize all agreed points — out loud, so both sides confirm the same deal
2Clarify any ambiguities — vague agreements become disputes later
3Put agreement in writing — even a one-line email counts
4Set clear next steps and deadlines — who does what, by when
5Thank the other party — preserve the relationship beyond the deal
6Follow up promptly — momentum from a good deal disappears fast

The AI Lesson to Land

Both parties got the same AI advice because they asked the same question: "How do I win?"

AI reflects the frame you give it. If you ask it to help you compete, it helps you compete. If you ask "What might the other side actually need from the oranges? Could there be a way we both get what we want?" — you get integrative thinking.

The quality of your AI use depends entirely on the quality of your questions. And the quality of your questions depends on how well you already understand negotiation. The tool amplifies the thinking. It doesn't replace it.

💡 To close both sessions: In Session 1, you learned that preparation wins in competitive negotiation. In Session 2, you learned that asking "why" wins in collaborative negotiation. The real skill is knowing which situation you're in — and AI can help you prepare for both, if you ask it the right questions.
